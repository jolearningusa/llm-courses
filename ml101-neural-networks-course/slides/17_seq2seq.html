Sequence to sequence

Sutskever, Vinyals and Le, 2014, and Cho et al., 2014. One recurrent network reads the input sequence into a vector; a second writes the output sequence from it. Trained end to end on sentence pairs, a deep LSTM matched a phrase-based translation system on English to French, and the encoder–decoder became the template for translation, summarization and speech recognition.

Problem: input and output of different lengths

  • Deck 15 maps a sequence to one label, or to one output per input step. Translation maps a sentence to a sentence of a different length, and word i of the output need not come from word i of the input.
  • Statistical machine translation (SMT) handled this with separate models for alignment, phrase tables and a language model, tuned together.
  • The goal: one network, trained end to end, that models p(y1…ym | x1…xn) for any n and m.

The encoder–decoder

p(y | x) = Πt p(yt | y1…yt−1, c) , c = the encoder's last state

The decoder is a language model (deck 15) whose initial state is c. It stops when it emits the end-of-sequence token.

Training and decoding

  • Teacher forcing: during training the decoder reads the correct previous token, not its own guess, so every output position can be scored with cross-entropy in one pass.
  • At test time it must read its own previous output. The mismatch (it never practised recovering from its own mistakes) is called exposure bias.
  • Greedy decoding takes the most likely token at each step. Beam search keeps the k most likely partial outputs and extends each; Sutskever et al. found a beam of 2 already gave most of the gain over greedy.
  • Lab L6 checks that greedy decoding gives the same tokens as teacher forcing fed with the model's own outputs.

Sutskever et al.: the model

  • WMT'14 English to French, 12 million sentence pairs. Vocabularies of the 160,000 most frequent English and 80,000 French words; the rest became UNK.
  • Separate encoder and decoder LSTMs, each with four layers of 1,000 cells and 1,000-dimensional word embeddings: 384 million parameters.
  • SGD without momentum at a learning rate of 0.7, halved every half epoch after the fifth, 7.5 epochs; batches of 128 sentences; gradient norm clipped at 5 (deck 15).
  • Eight GPUs, one per LSTM layer and four for the softmax; training took about ten days.
Sutskever, Vinyals and Le 2014, arXiv:1409.3215, §3.1, §3.4 and §3.5 · bibliography

A surprising trick: reverse the source

  • Feeding the source sentence backwards (C B A → W X Y) made the first target words close in time to the first source words they translate.
  • Test perplexity fell from 5.8 to 4.7, and the BLEU score of a single LSTM rose from 26.17 to 30.59.
  • The authors' explanation: reversal introduces many short-term dependencies, which make it easier for backpropagation to "establish communication" between source and target. The average distance between corresponding words is unchanged.
  • That a word-order trick mattered so much showed the weakness of the design: everything has to pass through one vector, and it is easiest to use what was read last.

The bottleneck, measured

  • Cho et al. analysed smaller encoder–decoders and found that their translation quality "degrades rapidly as the length of the sentence" grows. Sutskever et al.'s much larger reversed LSTM held up better on long sentences (their §3.7), but the limit is still there.

Where this shows up in LLMs

  • The original Transformer is an encoder–decoder for translation, and T5 casts every task as text to text with one. The decoder-only GPT models are the decoder half: the prompt plays the role of the source.
  • Teacher forcing is how every LLM is pre-trained: all positions are predicted in parallel from the true previous tokens.
  • Greedy decoding, beam search and sampling with a temperature are still the decoding choices; chat models mostly sample.
  • The fixed-vector bottleneck is the problem that attention (deck 18) removed.
