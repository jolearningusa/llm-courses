Momentum, RMSProp and Adam

Plain gradient descent is slow in narrow valleys and treats every parameter alike. Momentum fixes the first problem, per-parameter step sizes fix the second, and Adam combines both. Its decoupled-weight-decay variant, AdamW, trains nearly every LLM.

Problem: one step size for every direction

  • Deck 03: on a valley with curvatures 1 and κ, the learning rate must stay below 2/κ to avoid bouncing across the steep walls, and then crawls along the flat floor. Progress per step is about 1/κ.
  • Networks are worse: curvature differs by orders of magnitude between layers and parameters, and changes during training.
  • Gradients are also noisy (deck 03). A good optimizer should average out the noise, keep moving along consistent directions, and adapt to the scale of each parameter.
Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, Deep Learning, §8.2 and §8.3 · bibliography

Momentum: accumulate a velocity

v ← βv + ∇L(w) , w ← w − η v

  • The velocity is an exponentially weighted sum of past gradients. Components that keep their sign (along the valley floor) add up, to about 1/(1 − β) times one gradient; components that flip sign (across the valley) cancel.
  • Polyak's heavy-ball method (1964). With the best β and η on a quadratic, the number of steps grows like √κ instead of κ.
  • Nesterov momentum evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead point w − ηβv, which corrects an overshoot one step earlier.
  • Sutskever et al. showed that SGD with momentum, a well-scaled random initialization and a slowly increasing β trains deep autoencoders and recurrent networks to results that had needed second-order methods.
Sutskever et al. 2013, ICML, §2 and §3 · Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, Deep Learning, §8.3.2 · bibliography

Per-parameter step sizes: AdaGrad and RMSProp

s ← s + g² (AdaGrad) or s ← ρs + (1 − ρ) g² (RMSProp) ; w ← w − η g / (√s + ε)

  • Divide each coordinate's step by the root of its recent squared gradients: parameters with large or frequent gradients take smaller steps, rare ones take larger steps. Units do not matter: rescale a parameter and its step rescales with it.
  • AdaGrad sums all past squares, which suits sparse features, but its steps shrink like 1/√t forever (a lab L2 check), often too early for networks.
  • RMSProp uses a running average instead, so the step size tracks the recent gradient scale. Hinton introduced it in a 2012 lecture course, not a paper.
Duchi et al. 2011, JMLR · Hinton, Neural Networks for Machine Learning, lecture 6e · bibliography

Adam: momentum plus RMSProp

m ← β₁m + (1−β₁)g , v ← β₂v + (1−β₂)g² , w ← w − η · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε)

  • m is a running mean of the gradient (momentum), v a running mean of its square (RMSProp). Defaults from the paper: η = 0.001, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, ε = 10−8.
  • Bias correction: m and v start at zero, so early on they underestimate. m̂ = m/(1 − β₁t) and v̂ = v/(1 − β₂t) undo that. Without it, v is far more biased than m and the first step is η(1−β₁)/√(1−β₂) ≈ 3.2η instead of η (lab L2).
  • The step on each coordinate is roughly bounded by η, whatever the gradient's scale, which makes η easier to choose.
Kingma and Ba 2014, arXiv:1412.6980, §2 and §3, Algorithm 1 · bibliography

Adam in practice

  • In the paper's experiments (logistic regression, multilayer networks, convolutional networks on CIFAR-10) Adam converged as fast as or faster than SGD with momentum, AdaGrad and RMSProp.
  • On the Rosenbrock valley in lab L2 Adam reaches a loss of 10−30 in 5000 steps while plain gradient descent is still at 10−4.
  • It is not a free win: well-tuned SGD with momentum often generalized better on image classification, which motivated AdamW (next slide). With a constant learning rate, adaptive methods also keep jittering near the minimum; a decaying schedule (deck 09) finishes the job.
  • Memory: Adam stores m and v, two extra numbers per parameter.

AdamW: decouple the weight decay

  • For plain SGD, adding λ‖w‖²/2 to the loss and shrinking w by ηλ each step are the same thing. Inside Adam they are not: an L2 term added to the gradient gets divided by √v̂ like the rest of the gradient, so weights whose gradients are large are decayed much less, relative to their size, than weights whose gradients are small.
  • Loshchilov and Hutter apply the decay directly, w ← w − ηλw, outside the adaptive scaling. This improved Adam's generalization to be competitive with SGD with momentum on image classification, and made the best learning rate and decay factor more independent of each other.
  • Lab L2, decay only, 20 steps from w = [10, 0.1]: AdamW shrinks both weights by the same 18%; L2-in-Adam shrinks the large one by 20% and the small one by 73%.
Loshchilov and Hutter 2017, arXiv:1711.05101, §2 and §4 · Lab L2 · bibliography
8 / 10

Where this shows up in LLMs

  • AdamW is the default optimizer for Transformer language models. Llama 2, for example, used β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95, ε = 10−5, weight decay 0.1 and gradient clipping at 1.0.
  • β₂ = 0.95 rather than 0.999 lets the second-moment estimate react faster to gradient spikes, a common choice for stability in large runs.
  • The optimizer state is a large part of training memory: with mixed precision, weights, gradients and Adam's two moments together take several times the memory of the weights alone, which is why it is sharded across devices.
  • Newer optimizers (such as Muon, used in Kimi K2, Transformer course deck 33) are still compared against AdamW as the baseline.
Touvron et al. 2023, arXiv:2307.09288, §2.2 · Transformer course, deck 33 · bibliography
