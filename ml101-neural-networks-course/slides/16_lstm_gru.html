LSTM and GRU

Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997; Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins, 2000; Cho et al., 2014. Give the recurrent network a memory that is changed by addition rather than by repeated matrix products, and gates that decide what to write, keep and read. Gated networks could learn dependencies across hundreds of steps and became the standard sequence model until the Transformer.

Problem: plain RNNs forget

  • Deck 15: the gradient from step T to step t is a product of T − t Jacobians, so it vanishes exponentially. In lab L5 a plain RNN cannot solve the adding problem once the two numbers are 50 steps apart.
  • Hochreiter's 1991 thesis analysed the problem; Bengio et al. (1994) proved that gradient descent struggles with it whatever the training time.
  • The LSTM paper's answer: a unit whose state is carried forward by the identity, so the error flowing back through it neither shrinks nor grows, the constant error carousel. Gates protect it from irrelevant inputs and keep it from disturbing the rest of the network.

Why the cell state helps the gradient

  • Along the cell state, ∂ct/∂ct−1 = diag(f): no weight matrix and no squashing derivative. While the forget gate is near 1, the gradient passes back almost unchanged, as along a ResNet's identity shortcut (deck 14).
  • The 1997 LSTM had no forget gate: f = 1 always. It learned tasks with time lags of more than 1000 steps.
  • Gers et al. (2000) found that on long, unsegmented input streams the cell state of that LSTM could grow without bound, and added the forget gate so that the network can reset its memory. That is the LSTM used today.
  • Initialize the forget gate's bias to 1, so the cell remembers by default at the start of training: Jozefowicz et al. recommend it for every LSTM. Lab L5 does this.

The GRU

z = σ(x Wz + ht−1 Uz) , r = σ(x Wr + ht−1 Ur)
ĥ = tanh(x W + (r ⊙ ht−1) U) , ht = z ⊙ ht−1 + (1 − z) ⊙ ĥ

  • Cho et al. (2014) proposed this unit for their encoder–decoder. The update gate z interpolates between keeping the old state and writing the candidate; the reset gate r lets the unit ignore the old state when it computes the candidate.
  • No separate cell state and no output gate: three blocks of weights instead of four, so about 25% fewer parameters than an LSTM of the same width.
  • The path ht−1 → ht through z is additive, like the LSTM's cell.

Which gated unit?

  • Greff et al. (2015) compared the LSTM with eight variants in 5,400 runs on speech, handwriting and music. None improved significantly on the standard LSTM. Removing the forget gate or the output activation hurt most; coupling the input and forget gates, or removing peepholes, cost nothing. The learning rate mattered more than the variant.
  • Jozefowicz et al. (2015) searched over ten thousand architectures. The GRU beat the LSTM on every task except language modelling, and a forget-gate bias of 1 closed most of the gap.
  • Chung et al. (2014): gated units clearly beat the tanh RNN; between the GRU and LSTM they could not conclude.
  • In practice: either works; tune the learning rate first.

Where this shows up in LLMs

  • The additive memory path is the same idea as the residual stream: each Transformer block adds a correction to a running state instead of rewriting it (deck 14).
  • Multiplicative gates are back in every LLM's feed-forward block: SwiGLU multiplies one projection by a gated other (Transformer course deck 18).
  • Recurrent LLM layers are gated RNNs with a matrix-valued state: Mamba's input-dependent decay and Gated DeltaNet's forget gate decide, per token, how much of the state to keep (Transformer course decks 23 and 25).
  • Before 2017, the strongest language models and translation systems were stacks of LSTMs (decks 17 and 18).
