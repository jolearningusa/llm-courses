Generalization: overfitting, bias and variance

Low training loss is easy; what we want is low loss on data the model has never seen. This deck separates the two, shows how a flexible model fits noise, and ends with how deep networks complicate the classical story.

The goal is new data

  • Training minimizes the average loss on the training set. What matters is the expected loss on new examples from the same distribution, which we can only estimate.
  • Split the data three ways: training (fit the parameters), validation (choose hyperparameters: degree, λ, learning rate, when to stop), test (report once, at the end).
  • Every decision made by looking at a set leaks information from it. A test set used to choose between models is a validation set, and its score is optimistic.
  • The estimate assumes new data looks like the training data. When the distribution shifts, no split protects you.

  • Training error only falls as the degree rises, so it cannot choose the degree. The validation set can.

Bias and variance

E[(y − f̂(x))²] = σ² + (E[f̂(x)] − f(x))² + Var[f̂(x)]

  • Imagine drawing many training sets and fitting the model to each. At a point x, the expected squared error splits into noise σ² (no model removes it), bias² (how far the average fit is from the truth) and variance (how much the fit moves from one training set to the next).
  • Simple models: high bias, low variance. Flexible models: low bias, high variance. More data lowers the variance of the same model.
  • Regularization deliberately adds bias to cut variance: in the table, ridge raises the degree-12 training error ten-fold and lowers its validation error a thousand-fold.

Choosing hyperparameters

  • Parameters are learned by the optimizer (w). Hyperparameters are chosen around it: model size, λ, learning rate, batch size, number of steps.
  • Hold-out validation: train each candidate, keep the one with the lowest validation loss.
  • k-fold cross-validation: for small data, rotate which fold is held out and average. It costs k fits per candidate, so it is rare for large networks.
  • Early stopping: treat the number of training steps as a hyperparameter and keep the checkpoint with the best validation loss (deck 10).

Deep networks bend the classical picture

  • They can memorize. Zhang et al. trained standard image classifiers on CIFAR-10 with the labels replaced by random ones: the networks still reached zero training error. Capacity alone cannot explain why the same networks generalize on real labels.
  • Double descent. Belkin et al. observed that test error, after rising toward the point where the model just interpolates the training data, can fall again as capacity grows further. Nakkiran et al. found the same in modern networks as a function of width and of training time.
  • The U-curve is still the right picture at a fixed, moderate size. For very large models, the practical lesson is to measure validation loss rather than reason from parameter counts.

Where this shows up in LLMs

  • Pre-training usually sees each token once or a few times, so the gap between training and held-out loss is small; the limit is data and compute, which the scaling laws model (Transformer course decks 05 and 06).
  • Fine-tuning on a small set is classical territory again: a few epochs can overfit, and validation loss decides when to stop.
  • Benchmark contamination is test-set leakage: if test questions appear in the pre-training data, the score measures memory, not generalization.
  • The habit transfers unchanged: keep a held-out set you never train or tune on.
