Regularization: weight decay, dropout, early stopping, augmentation

A network with more parameters than training examples can memorize them, noise included (deck 04). Regularization is anything that makes the learned function simpler or more robust without making the model smaller. Four tools cover most practice, and one lab measures all of them on the same problem.

The test bed: a network that memorizes

  • Lab L3: 40 training points from two interleaved half-moons, with 10% of the labels flipped; 2000 clean validation points; an MLP 2 → 256 → 256 → 2 (about 67,000 parameters); 4000 full-batch SGD steps.
  • Unregularized, it reaches 100% training accuracy, which means it has also learned the four flipped labels.
  • Its validation loss is lowest at step 200 (0.357) and then climbs to 0.809: the classic overfitting curve. Its validation accuracy ends at 89.7%.
  • The question for every method below: does it lower the validation loss, and does the network stop fitting the flipped labels?

Weight decay

w ← w − η (∇L + λw) ⇔ minimize L + (λ/2)‖w‖²

  • Shrink every weight toward zero a little at each step. For SGD this is exactly an L2 penalty (deck 01's ridge), and the lab checks the equivalence. For Adam it is not, so use AdamW (deck 08).
  • Small weights make the function smoother: large weights are what let a network carve a sharp boundary around a single noisy point.
  • Apply it to weight matrices, not to biases or normalization gains, whose size does not create that kind of complexity.
  • Lab L3, λ = 10−2: validation loss 0.223 at the end instead of 0.809, accuracy 93.6%; the weight norm falls from 34 to 5, and training accuracy is 95%: it no longer fits the flipped labels.

Early stopping

  • Evaluate on the validation set during training and keep the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss. Training length becomes a hyperparameter chosen for free in one run.
  • For a quadratic loss and gradient descent, stopping after a fixed number of steps has the same effect as an L2 penalty whose strength falls as the number of steps grows.
  • In lab L3 the unregularized net's best checkpoint (step 200) has validation loss 0.357, less than half its final 0.809.
  • Note the disagreement: at step 200 its accuracy is 87.0%, lower than the final 89.7%. Later, the network becomes overconfident: it gets more points right but is very wrong on the ones it misses. Decide in advance which number you stop on.

Dropout

  • During training, set each hidden unit to zero with probability p, independently for every example and every step. At test time, use all units.
  • Inverted dropout scales the surviving units by 1/(1 − p) during training, so the expected activation is unchanged and nothing needs rescaling at test time. (The paper instead scales the weights at test time; the two are equivalent.)
  • Why it works: a unit cannot rely on any particular other unit being present, which discourages co-adapted feature detectors. Training samples one of exponentially many thinned networks per step; the test-time network approximately averages them.
  • The paper's guidance: retain 50% to 80% of hidden units and about 80% of input units.
Hinton et al. 2012, arXiv:1207.0580 · Srivastava et al. 2014, JMLR, §2, §4 and Appendix A · bibliography

Data augmentation

  • Make more training data by transforming the examples you have in ways that should not change the label: crops, flips, small rotations, color changes for images; noise or time shifts for audio.
  • It is the most direct fix for overfitting, because it attacks the cause (too little data) and encodes what you know about the task (a cat flipped left to right is still a cat).
  • AlexNet trained on random 224 × 224 crops of 256 × 256 images and their horizontal reflections, plus a color perturbation; without this, the authors say, the network would have overfit substantially.
  • The transformation must truly preserve the label: flipping a "6" or a "b" changes it.
Krizhevsky et al. 2012, NeurIPS, §4.1 · Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, Deep Learning, §7.4 · bibliography

Other regularizers you will meet

  • Label smoothing: train toward a target that puts 1 − ε on the correct class and spreads ε over the others, so the network is never pushed to infinite confidence. The 2017 Transformer used ε = 0.1.
  • Noise injection: add noise to inputs or weights; dropout is one structured form.
  • Parameter sharing: convolutional and recurrent networks use the same weights at every position (decks 12 and 15), a strong built-in constraint.
  • Normalization layers (deck 11) and the noise of small batches (deck 09) also have a regularizing effect, though that is not why they are used.
Vaswani et al. 2017, arXiv:1706.03762, §5.4 · Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, Deep Learning, §7.5 and §7.9 · bibliography

Where this shows up in LLMs

  • The 2017 Transformer used dropout 0.1 on every sub-layer output and on the embeddings, and label smoothing 0.1.
  • Large pre-training runs see each token about once, so overfitting is not the main risk. Many recent LLM reports use no dropout in pre-training but keep weight decay: Llama 2 used AdamW with weight decay 0.1.
  • Fine-tuning on a small dataset is classical territory again: dropout, weight decay, a few epochs and early stopping on validation loss.
  • Data augmentation has text analogues: paraphrasing, back-translation, and synthetic data generated by other models.
Vaswani et al. 2017, arXiv:1706.03762, §5.4 · Touvron et al. 2023, arXiv:2307.09288, §2.2 · bibliography
