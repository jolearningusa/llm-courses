Initialization and vanishing gradients

Glorot and Bengio, 2010, and He et al., 2015. A deep network multiplies its input by one weight matrix per layer, and its gradient by the same matrices on the way back. Choose their scale badly and the signal shrinks or explodes exponentially with depth before training has even started.

Problem: deep networks that do not train

  • Before 2006–2010, deep fully connected networks trained from random weights often did worse than shallow ones; the common fix was layer-wise unsupervised pre-training.
  • A ten-layer ReLU network with weights drawn from N(0, 0.01²), a once-common default, has activations of order 10−10 at its last hidden layer in lab L1. The gradient reaching the first layer is of order 10−14: the early layers effectively never move.
  • With weights a little too large, the same products grow instead and the loss overflows.
  • The question: what scale keeps the signal near 1 at every depth, forward and backward?

Variance through one layer

y = Σi=1..nin wi xi ⇒ Var(y) = nin · Var(w) · Var(x)

  • Assume weights and inputs are independent with zero mean. Each layer multiplies the variance by nin Var(w), so after L layers the factor is (nin Var(w))L.
  • To keep the forward signal steady: Var(w) = 1/nin. The backward signal passes through Wᵀ, so it wants Var(w) = 1/nout.
  • Xavier (Glorot) initialization takes the compromise Var(w) = 2/(nin + nout), for example uniform on ±√(6/(nin + nout)). It assumes an activation that is roughly linear near 0, like tanh.

Glorot and Bengio: what went wrong, and the fix

  • They monitored activations and gradients layer by layer during training. With sigmoid units and the then-standard init, the top hidden layer saturated near 0 early in training and was slow to recover.
  • Zero-centered activations (tanh, softsign) behaved better than the sigmoid, whose outputs average 0.5.
  • With the standard init, back-propagated gradients became smaller toward the lower layers; with the normalized init the variance of activations and gradients stayed similar across layers, and training was faster and reached lower error.
Glorot and Bengio 2010, AISTATS, §3 and §4 · bibliography

He initialization: correct for the ReLU

Var(w) = 2 / nin

  • A ReLU sets about half its inputs to zero, so it halves the second moment passed to the next layer. Doubling the weight variance compensates exactly.
  • He et al. trained a 30-layer ReLU network from scratch on ImageNet with this initialization; with Xavier initialization the same network stalled and did not learn. At 22 layers both converged, theirs sooner.
  • The same paper introduced the PReLU, a ReLU with a learned negative slope, and became the default initialization for ReLU networks (PyTorch calls it Kaiming initialization).

Vanishing and exploding gradients in general

  • The gradient at layer 1 is a product of one Jacobian per layer above it. If their typical scale is below 1 the product vanishes; above 1 it explodes. Depth turns a small mismatch into an exponential one.
  • Saturating activations make it worse: the sigmoid's derivative is at most 1/4.
  • Recurrent networks apply the same matrix at every time step, so the problem appears over time rather than depth. Bengio, Simard and Frasconi showed in 1994 why gradient descent struggles to learn long-range dependencies this way (deck 15).
  • Remedies, each a later deck: good initialization (here), ReLU-like activations (05), normalization layers (11), residual connections (14), gating (16) and gradient clipping (15).
Bengio, Simard and Frasconi 1994, doi:10.1109/72.279181 · Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, Deep Learning, §8.2.5 · bibliography

Details that matter

  • Break symmetry. If all weights in a layer start equal, every unit computes the same thing and receives the same gradient, forever. Random initialization is what lets units specialize. Biases can start at zero.
  • Uniform or Gaussian matters less than the variance. Some libraries use a truncated normal to avoid rare large weights.
  • The last layer is often initialized small, or to zero, so initial predictions are near uniform and the starting loss is log k (deck 02).
  • Initialization is not the whole story. Normalization layers (deck 11) and residual connections (deck 14) make networks far less sensitive to it, which is how 100-layer networks became routine.
Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, Deep Learning, §8.4 · bibliography

Where this shows up in LLMs

  • GPT-1 used a plain N(0, 0.02²) initialization, noting that with LayerNorm throughout the model that was sufficient; GPT-2 and many later models keep 0.02 as a default.
  • GPT-2 also scaled the weights of residual layers at initialization by 1/√N, where N is the number of residual layers, so that the sum of N residual branches does not grow with depth.
  • Pre-LN placement, RMSNorm and QK-norm (Transformer course deck 17) are the descendants of the same concern: keep activations and gradients at a stable scale in a stack of 30 to 100+ blocks.
