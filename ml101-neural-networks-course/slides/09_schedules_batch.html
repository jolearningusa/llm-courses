Learning-rate schedules and batch size

The learning rate is the most important hyperparameter, and the best value changes during training: small at the very start, large while the model is far from good, small again at the end. The batch size is tied to it. This deck covers the schedules in use and how to change one when you change the other.

Why decay: the noise floor

  • With stochastic gradients, a constant learning rate does not converge to the minimum: the iterate keeps bouncing in a region whose size grows with η and with the gradient noise.
  • Decaying η shrinks that region. This is the practical form of the Robbins–Monro condition from deck 03 (Σ ηt = ∞, Σ ηt² < ∞).
  • Lab L2, noisy least squares, batch 8, 3000 steps, same peak rate 0.02. Squared error of the weights at the end:

0.02430.00340.00400.0052

Every decaying schedule ends five to seven times closer than the constant one; the differences between them are small here.

Step decay and cosine

  • Step decay: divide by 10 when the validation error plateaus, or at fixed points. ResNet on CIFAR-10 started at 0.1 and divided by 10 at 32k and 48k iterations, stopping at 64k.
  • Cosine annealing: ηt = ηmin + ½(ηmax − ηmin)(1 + cos(π t / T)). Introduced with warm restarts (SGDR); without restarts it is a smooth decay with one hyperparameter less than step decay (no drop points).
  • Either way, most of the progress in the final phase comes from the decay itself: loss curves often drop sharply right after a step down.

Warm-up

  • At initialization the gradients can be large and Adam's second-moment estimate is based on few samples. A full learning rate in the first steps can push the model into a bad region it never leaves.
  • Linear warm-up ramps η from near 0 to its peak over the first steps (a few hundred to a few thousand), then hands over to the decay.
  • The 2017 Transformer used 4000 warm-up steps and then η ∝ 1/√step. Post-LN Transformers barely trained without warm-up (Transformer course deck 17).
  • Goyal et al. needed a gradual warm-up over 5 epochs to train ResNet-50 with very large batches without losing accuracy.

Batch size and the learning rate

  • A batch k times larger averages k times more gradients: the noise variance drops by k, and the same number of epochs means k times fewer steps.
  • Linear scaling rule: when the batch is multiplied by k, multiply the learning rate by k. With it and warm-up, Goyal et al. trained ResNet-50 with batches of 8192 images on 256 GPUs in one hour, matching the accuracy of batch 256.
  • The rule breaks down past some size. McCandlish et al. relate that critical batch size to the gradient noise scale: below it, doubling the batch nearly halves the steps; above it, extra batch buys little.
  • Keskar et al. found that very large batches tend to converge to sharp minima that generalize worse, part of why the noise of small batches is sometimes called a regularizer.
Goyal et al. 2017, arXiv:1706.02677, §2 · McCandlish et al. 2018, arXiv:1812.06162 · Keskar et al. 2016, arXiv:1609.04836 · bibliography

Finding the learning rate

  • Sweep η on a log grid (factors of 2 to 3) for a short run and plot the loss. The best value is usually a little below the one where training becomes unstable.
  • Deck 03's rule of thumb holds in spirit: there is a sharp edge (2/λmax for a quadratic) beyond which loss explodes; good rates sit near it.
  • Tune η again whenever you change the optimizer, the batch size, the model width or the normalization.
  • Random search over several hyperparameters beats a grid when only a few of them matter (deck 19).

Gradient clipping

  • A single bad batch or a region of high curvature can produce a gradient many times the usual size. One such step can undo hours of training.
  • Clip by global norm: if ‖g‖ > c, rescale g to norm c. The direction is kept; only the length is capped.
  • Introduced for recurrent networks, whose gradients can explode over time (deck 15), and standard in Transformer training (c = 1.0 is common).
  • Watch the gradient norm during training: frequent clipping means the learning rate is too high or something is unstable.

Where this shows up in LLMs

  • The standard LLM recipe is linear warm-up followed by cosine decay. Llama 2 used 2000 warm-up steps and decayed to 10% of the peak learning rate, with gradient clipping at 1.0.
  • Batch size is measured in tokens and is sometimes ramped up early in training (GPT-3 grew it over the first 4 to 12 billion tokens); the peak learning rate is chosen with the batch, and larger models usually get smaller peak rates.
  • Some recent runs replace cosine with a constant rate followed by a short final decay ("warm-up, stable, decay", as in MiniCPM), so a run can be extended without restarting the schedule.
  • Every one of these is a choice of ηt made for the reasons on slides 2 to 6.
