Recurrent networks and backpropagation through time

Elman, 1990; Werbos, 1990; Bengio, Simard and Frasconi, 1994; Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio, 2012. A recurrent network reads a sequence one step at a time and carries a hidden state forward, with the same weights at every step. Training it is backpropagation on the unrolled network, and it shows why plain RNNs forget: the gradient is multiplied by the same matrix once per step.

Problem: inputs that are sequences

  • Text, speech, prices and sensor readings come as sequences of varying length. An MLP needs a fixed-size input, and a fixed window cuts off everything older than the window.
  • What happens at step 5 should be recognized at step 50 too. Deck 12 shared weights across positions in an image; a recurrent network shares them across time.
  • Elman (1990) gave a network "context units" that hold a copy of the previous hidden state. Trained to predict the next letter of a text without spaces, its prediction error rose at the start of each word and fell inside it: it had found word boundaries without being told.

The recurrence

ht = tanh(xtWx + ht−1Wh + b) , yt = htWo + bo

Read out on every step (a language model), only on the last (classify a sequence), or feed the last state to a second network (deck 17). The parameter count does not depend on the sequence length.

Backpropagation through time

  • Unroll the network over the sequence and it is an ordinary deep network whose layers share weights. Backpropagate through it as in deck 06, from the last step to the first.
  • The gradient reaching ht has two parts: from the output at step t, and from step t + 1:

∂L/∂ht = ∂L/∂yt · WoT + ∂L/∂ht+1 · diag(1 − ht+12) WhT

  • Each step adds its contribution to the same ∂L/∂Wh, ∂L/∂Wx and ∂L/∂b. Memory grows with T, since every step's activations are kept for the backward pass.
  • Truncated BPTT backpropagates through at most k steps but still carries the state forward: cheaper, but blind to dependencies longer than k.

Why the gradient vanishes or explodes

  • From step T back to step t the gradient is multiplied by T − t factors diag(1 − h²) WhT. In a linear network this is WhT−t: its size goes like λT−t, with λ the largest eigenvalue magnitude.
  • Pascanu et al.: if λ < 1/γ, where γ bounds the activation's derivative (1 for tanh, 1/4 for the sigmoid), long-term contributions vanish exponentially. λ > 1/γ is necessary for them to explode.
  • Bengio et al. (1994) showed the dilemma: to store information robustly the network must sit where gradients from the distant past are small. So learning long-range dependencies by gradient descent is hard, not just slow.
Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio 2012, arXiv:1211.5063, §2 · Bengio, Simard and Frasconi 1994, doi:10.1109/72.279181 · bibliography

Exploding gradients: clip them

if ‖g‖ > τ : g ← g · τ / ‖g‖

  • Pascanu et al. proposed rescaling the whole gradient whenever its norm passes a threshold τ. The direction is kept; only the step is shortened when the loss surface has a cliff.
  • The norm is taken over all parameters together ("global norm"), so the relative sizes of the parameter gradients do not change.
  • Clipping does nothing for vanishing gradients. For those the fix had to be in the architecture: gates (deck 16) and, later, attention (deck 18).
  • Lab L5 clips every network at τ = 1 with clip_by_global_norm.

Language modelling with an RNN

  • Read the text one character at a time and predict the next one at every step: the loss is the cross-entropy of deck 02, averaged over all positions.
  • To generate, sample a character from the softmax, feed it back in as the next input, repeat. A temperature T divides the logits before the softmax: T → 0 is greedy, large T is closer to uniform.
  • Lab L5 trains an LSTM on a 534-character paragraph (26 symbols). The loss falls from ln 26 = 3.26 to 0.12 nats per character: it has memorized the text. Greedy decoding from "the gra" reproduces the paragraph; at T = 2 it drifts into "the gradieng samadients of ate wrid…".
  • Graves (2013) trained such networks on Wikipedia text and handwriting and showed they could generate long, plausible sequences.

Where this shows up in LLMs

  • Next-token prediction with a cross-entropy at every position, and generation by sampling one token at a time with a temperature, are exactly how LLMs are trained and used. Only the network that computes the logits changed.
  • An RNN squeezes the past into a fixed-size state. A Transformer keeps every past token's keys and values (the KV cache), which grows with the context. That trade-off between a fixed memory and exact recall comes back throughout the Transformer course.
  • Linear-attention and state-space models such as Mamba and Gated DeltaNet are recurrent networks again: a fixed-size state updated once per token, but designed so that training runs in parallel over the sequence (Transformer course decks 23 and 25).
  • Gradient clipping at a global norm of 1.0 is standard in LLM training.
