Learning from human preferences

Some goals are easy to judge and hard to write down: a good backflip, a good summary. Christiano et al. (2017) learned a reward model from people's pairwise choices and trained a policy on it. Ziegler et al. (2019) and Stiennon et al. (2020) carried the method to language models, where it beat supervised fine-tuning on summaries.

Problem: the goal has no reward function

  • Reinforcement learning needs a reward. For many tasks people can recognize a good result but cannot write a formula for it.
  • Demonstrations help, but a policy that copies them cannot do better than the demonstrator, and some behaviours are hard to demonstrate.
  • Asking for a score on an absolute scale gives inconsistent numbers. Asking which of two results is better is easier and more consistent.
  • Idea: learn a reward model from comparisons, then optimize the policy against the model, asking people about only a small fraction of what the policy does.

Christiano et al.: preferences over short clips

  • People compared pairs of 1–2 second clips from Atari games and simulated robots. The policy, the reward model and the queries were all updated together, online.
  • Queries went to the pairs where an ensemble of reward models disagreed most, a cheap measure of uncertainty.

The Bradley-Terry reward model

P(y1 ≻ y2) = σ(r(y1) − r(y2))    L = − log σ(r(yw) − r(yl))
  • The probability of preferring one result grows with the difference of rewards (a clip's reward is the sum over its frames). Training minimizes the cross-entropy of the human labels.
  • Only differences are identified: adding a constant to r changes nothing, so reward models are normalized afterwards.
  • Christiano et al. assumed a 10% chance that a person answers at random, ensembled 3 reward models, and normalized the reward to a fixed standard deviation.
  • Lab L5 fits this model to synthetic preferences and checks it on held-out pairs.

Evidence: little human time, but it must stay online

  • Feedback on less than 1% of the agent's interactions was enough for most Atari games and robot tasks, with 30 minutes to 5 hours of human time per task.
  • A simulated robot learned a backflip, a behaviour with no hand-written reward, from 900 queries in under an hour of a person's time.
  • On some tasks the learned reward did as well as or better than the true reward, because it shaped learning better.
  • Offline fails: a reward model trained once on a fixed set of comparisons was exploited. The policy found behaviour the model scored highly and people would not, so queries had to follow the policy.

Ziegler et al.: the same loop on GPT-2

R(x, y) = r(x, y) − β log π(y | x) / ρ(y | x)
  • GPT-2 774M. Labelers chose the best of 4 continuations. The reward is penalized by the KL divergence from the pre-trained model ρ, so the policy cannot drift into text the reward model has never seen.
  • Style: positive sentiment and vivid description learned from 5,000 comparisons.
  • Summarization with 60,000 comparisons produced "smart copiers": the RL policy copied whole sentences from the article in 71% of TL;DR and 98% of CNN/Daily Mail summaries. Labelers liked that, and it did not beat a lead-3 baseline.
  • Collecting labels online, from the current policy, mattered for summarization.
Ziegler et al. 2019, arXiv:1909.08593 §2–4 · bibliography

Stiennon et al.: summaries better than the references

  • TL;DR Reddit posts, 64,832 comparisons collected in batches from the current policies. A 6.7B reward model initialized from the SFT model, then PPO against it with a KL penalty to the SFT policy.
  • Close work with labelers: agreement between labelers and researchers was 77 ± 2%, after training and feedback through a shared chat.
  • Evidence: a 1.3B RLHF model was preferred over a supervised model ten times its size. The 6.7B RLHF summaries scored 7 of 7 for overall quality 45% of the time, against 20% for SFT and 23% for the human-written references.
  • The policy transferred to CNN/Daily Mail news without news training and was nearly as good as a model trained on it.
Stiennon et al. 2020, arXiv:2009.01325 §3–4, Figs. 1, 4 · bibliography

Reward models scale, then get exploited

  • Reward-model accuracy rose about 1.1% per doubling of comparisons and 1.8% per doubling of model size.
  • Overoptimization: optimizing harder against the reward model first raised human preference, then lowered it; past some KL, the reward model's scores and human judgement were anti-correlated. Deck 20 measures this.
  • Fine-tuning the 6.7B policy with RL took about 320 GPU-days.
Stiennon et al. 2020, arXiv:2009.01325 §4.3, Fig. 5–6, App. D · bibliography

Training impact and afterlife

  • The reward model is the interface. People label comparisons; everything after that is optimization against a learned model, which is why its errors matter (deck 20).
  • The KL penalty to a reference model from Ziegler et al. stayed in every RLHF recipe, and it is the starting point of DPO's derivation (deck 22).
  • Online data matters: Christiano and Ziegler found it, Anthropic and Llama 2 collected comparisons from each new model version (deck 20).
  • PPO, the optimizer used here, and the full InstructGPT pipeline come next (deck 19).
