LLM Training and Post-Training

How a language model is trained, from raw web text to a chat and reasoning model, one paper at a time: what each method optimizes, what it needs, and what it changed in the models that followed.

Who this course is for

  • Engineers and students who can read a model card and want to know how each training stage behind it works, what it costs and when it fails.
  • Prerequisites: Python, NumPy or PyTorch, probability, and basic neural-network training: a loss, backpropagation, SGD, softmax and cross-entropy.
  • Not covered: classic networks (MLPs, CNNs, RNNs, LSTMs) and the basics of training them; they are taught in ML 101: Neural Network Foundations.
  • You should know what a decoder-only Transformer computes, at the level of architecture deck 01 and 02.

How every paper deck is built

  1. Problem. What was failing, too expensive or missing when the paper was written.
  2. Method. The objective, data or recipe, with the equation or diagram that defines it.
  3. Evidence. What the paper measured, at what scale, against which baseline.
  4. Training impact. Effect on quality, compute, data needed, memory and stability, and what it made possible.
  5. Afterlife. Which later reports kept, changed or dropped it.

Close sequels share a deck (FLAN and the Flan Collection; the DPO variants). Survey decks such as 09 give the training view of a topic the compute course teaches in depth.

How to use the decks

  • Two or three decks a week over 15 weeks, then the lab. Read the paper sections named on each slide before class.
  • Two shorter courses: pre-training (Parts 0 to IV, weeks 1 to 6) and post-training (Parts V to XI, weeks 7 to 15), which needs only deck 01 and lab L1.
  • When a slide cites a number, it gives the table or section. Check it there.
  • Assessment: labs 50%, two paper critiques 20%, capstone 30%. The capstone plans a training run from base model to chat model for a given compute, data and memory budget.
