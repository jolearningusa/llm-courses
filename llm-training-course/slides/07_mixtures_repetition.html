Data mixtures and repetition

A corpus is several sources, and a token budget decides how often each is read. DoReMi and RegMix choose the mixture with small models; Muennighoff et al. measure what repeating data is worth when unique text runs out.

Problem: how much of each source, and how many times

  • The Pile set its weights by hand, repeating "higher-quality" components: PubMed Central and ArXiv twice, Pile-CC once.
  • A mixture is a vector of weights over domains; the best one depends on the model and the evaluation, and testing each candidate on the full model is unaffordable.
  • When the budget exceeds the unique data in a domain (math, code, a low-resource language), the only options are to repeat it or go without. Chinchilla's law assumes every token is new (architecture deck 06).

DoReMi: weights from a robust proxy

  1. Train a 280M reference model on default weights.
  2. Train a 280M proxy with Group DRO: at each step raise the weight of domains where the proxy's loss exceeds the reference's most (excess loss). Average the weights over training.
  3. Train the main model, 8B in the paper (30× larger), on the new weights.

Finding the weights cost about 8% of the 8B model's training FLOPs.

RegMix: fit a regression over many tiny runs

  • Train 512 models of 1M parameters for 1B tokens each, on random mixtures; fit a gradient-boosted regressor from mixture weights to validation loss; search a million mixtures in seconds; train on the average of the top 100.
  • The search cost about 10% of DoReMi's (3.5 × 1018 against 3.7 × 1019 FLOPs). On a 1B model with 25B tokens, average score 47.3 against DoReMi's 46.8 and a human-chosen mixture's 45.1.
  • Mixture rankings held from 1M to 1B parameters (Spearman ρ 97%); larger scales were not tested.
  • Web text (Pile-CC) loss predicted downstream scores better than Wikipedia loss, and domain interactions were hard to guess by hand.

Repetition: what a second epoch is worth

Effective data D′ = U + U·R*·(1 − e−R/R*), with U unique tokens, R = epochs − 1 and R* ≈ 15.39, fitted on 182 of the paper's 400+ runs (up to 9B parameters and 900B tokens). Repeats approach a ceiling of U(1 + R*).

Evidence: four epochs are nearly free

  • An 8.7B model trained for 4 epochs on 44B unique tokens ended with 0.5% higher validation loss than 1 epoch on 178B unique tokens; downstream differences were insignificant.
  • Past about 16 epochs value falls off fast; at 40 epochs extra repeats are worth nothing.
  • Excess parameters lose value faster than repeated data (R*N ≈ 5.3 against R*D ≈ 15.4): with limited data, spend extra compute on epochs before size.
  • Mixing in up to 50% Python code did not hurt natural-language tasks, about doubling usable tokens; perplexity filtering helped, deduplication did not when data was scarce.

Training impact and afterlife

  • Mixtures are tuned, not guessed. Llama 3 chose its mix with knowledge classification (downsampling over-represented web categories) and scaling-law experiments on small models: about 50% general knowledge, 25% math and reasoning, 17% code, 8% multilingual.
  • Proxies decide. DoReMi, RegMix and Llama 3 all assume that a mixture that wins on small models wins on large ones. RegMix checked this up to 1B; the assumption is the weak point.
  • Repetition is a budget tool. Rare domains are repeated a few epochs, and code stretches scarce text. Lab L2 computes epochs per domain and the value of repeats.
