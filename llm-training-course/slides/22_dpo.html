Direct Preference Optimization

RLHF fits a reward model and then runs RL against it. Rafailov et al. (2023) showed that the KL-regularized RLHF objective has a closed-form optimum, so the reward can be written in terms of the policy itself. Substituting it into the Bradley-Terry likelihood gives a classification loss on preference pairs: no reward model, no sampling and no RL.

Problem: RLHF is a heavy, fragile pipeline

  • PPO keeps a policy, a value model, a reference and a reward model, and generates text inside the training loop (deck 19).
  • It has many hyperparameters and is unstable; the reward model can be exploited (deck 20).
  • Question: both stages optimize the same objective in the end. Can the policy be trained on the preference data directly?

Step 1: the RLHF objective has a closed-form optimum

maxπ Ex, y∼π[r(x, y)] − β KL(π(·|x) ‖ πref(·|x))
πr(y | x) = πref(y | x) exp(r(x, y) / β) / Z(x)
  • The same objective PPO optimizes with the KL penalty (decks 18–19). Its maximizer over all policies tilts the reference by the exponentiated reward; Z(x) normalizes over all answers and cannot be computed.
  • Taking logs and rearranging: r(x, y) = β log πr(y|x) / πref(y|x) + β log Z(x). Any reward corresponds to a policy, and the policy determines the reward up to a function of x.
Rafailov et al. 2023 (DPO), arXiv:2305.18290 §4, Eqs. 3–5, App. A.1 · bibliography

Step 2: substitute into Bradley-Terry

LDPO = −E log σ( r̂(x, yw) − r̂(x, yl) )
r̂(x, y) = β log πθ(y|x) / πref(y|x)
  • Bradley-Terry depends only on the difference of two rewards for the same prompt, so β log Z(x) cancels. What is left is the reward model's loss written in terms of the policy (deck 18).
  • Training needs two forward passes per answer: the policy (trained) and the reference (frozen, usually the SFT model). Log-probabilities of the reference can be computed once in advance.
  • "Your language model is secretly a reward model": β log πθ/πref is an implicit reward. Every reward class under Bradley-Terry has exactly one such representative.
Rafailov et al. 2023 (DPO), arXiv:2305.18290 §4–5.1, Eqs. 6–7, Thm. 1 · bibliography

What the gradient does

∇L = −β E[ σ(r̂l − r̂w) (∇log π(yw) − ∇log π(yl)) ]
  • r̂w, r̂l are the implicit rewards of the two answers. Raise the chosen answer's likelihood, lower the rejected one's, weighted by how wrongly the implicit reward orders the pair. Pairs already ordered correctly get little weight.
  • Without the weight, the paper reports that the model degenerates. The weight and β are what keep the policy near the reference.
  • Lab L6 checks that this gradient, trained on Bradley-Terry preferences, lands on πref exp(r/β)/Z to 10−6.

Evidence: DPO

  • Controlled sentiment (GPT-2 Large, IMDb): DPO had the best reward-against-KL frontier, above PPO and even above PPO given the true reward.
  • Summarization (TL;DR, GPT-J): about 61% win rate against reference summaries at temperature 0, against about 57% for PPO at its best temperature, and DPO was less sensitive to temperature.
  • Dialogue (Anthropic HH, Pythia 2.8B): the only efficient method that improved on the preferred completions in the data.
  • Out of distribution: on CNN/Daily Mail, 0.36 against PPO's 0.26 at temperature 0.
  • GPT-4 judged the comparisons; it agreed with humans about as often as humans agreed with each other.
Rafailov et al. 2023 (DPO), arXiv:2305.18290 §6.1–6.4, Fig. 2–3, Tables 1–2 · bibliography

In practice

  • Settings in the paper: β = 0.1 (0.5 for TL;DR), batch size 64, RMSprop, learning rate 10−6 with 150 warm-up steps.
  • β sets the strength of the KL constraint: small β lets the policy move far from the reference.
  • Offline: the pairs are fixed in advance, often written by other models. DPO never sees its own samples, which is the subject of decks 23 and 24.
  • What to monitor: preference accuracy of the implicit reward on held-out pairs, the reward margin, and the log-probabilities of the chosen and rejected answers, which can both fall while the margin grows.

Training impact and afterlife

  • Preference tuning became cheap: a few lines of loss on top of an SFT trainer, at a cost close to SFT. Open models adopted it quickly; Tülu 3 used DPO as its preference stage (deck 15).
  • The derivation became a template: IPO, KTO, ORPO and SimPO change the loss and keep the idea of training on preferences directly (deck 23).
  • The doubts: DPO can put probability on answers the reference never produced, overfits deterministic preferences, and depends on how close the data is to the policy. Comparisons with well-tuned PPO are deck 24.
