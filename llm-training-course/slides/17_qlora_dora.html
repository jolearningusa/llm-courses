QLoRA, DoRA, and what LoRA learns and forgets

QLoRA (2023) stores the frozen model in 4 bits and fine-tunes a 65B model on one 48 GB GPU. DoRA (2024) splits each weight into a magnitude and a direction to close part of LoRA's gap to full fine-tuning. Biderman et al. (2024) measured that gap and found its other side: LoRA also forgets less.

Problem: LoRA still holds the whole model in 16 bits

  • LoRA removes optimizer state and gradients for frozen weights, but the frozen weights still sit in GPU memory: 130 GB for a 65B model in 16 bits.
  • Full 16-bit fine-tuning of a 65B model needs over 780 GB.
  • Two questions follow: can the frozen model be stored in 4 bits while the update is still learned at full quality, and how close is LoRA to full fine-tuning in the first place?

QLoRA: 4-bit NormalFloat

  • Trained weights are roughly normal. NF4 places its 16 levels at quantiles of N(0, 1), scaled to [−1, 1], so each level is used about equally often; Int4 spaces them evenly.
  • Asymmetric on purpose: 8 levels above zero, 7 below, and an exact 0. Each block of 64 weights is scaled by its absolute maximum.
Dettmers et al. 2023 (QLoRA), arXiv:2305.14314 §3, App. E · bibliography

QLoRA: double quantization and paged optimizers

  • Double quantization: one FP32 scale per 64 weights costs 0.5 bits per parameter. Quantizing those scales to 8 bits in blocks of 256 cuts it to 0.127 bits.
  • Paged optimizers: optimizer state lives in unified memory and moves to the CPU when the GPU runs out, absorbing memory spikes on long sequences.
  • Compute: weights are dequantized to BF16 for each matrix multiply; gradients flow through the frozen 4-bit weights into LoRA adapters on every linear layer.
  • Adapting only Wq and Wv did not match full fine-tuning on large models; adapting all linear layers did, and then the rank r mattered little.
Dettmers et al. 2023 (QLoRA), arXiv:2305.14314 §3–4 · bibliography

DoRA: train the magnitude and the direction

W′ = m · (W0 + BA) / ‖W0 + BA‖c
  • Write each weight as a magnitude vector m (one value per column) times unit-norm columns. Train m directly and the direction with LoRA; ‖·‖c is the column-wise norm.
  • Why: in full fine-tuning, changes in magnitude and direction were negatively correlated (−0.62); in LoRA positively (+0.83); DoRA −0.31, closer to full fine-tuning.
  • Evidence: LLaMA-7B commonsense reasoning average 78.4 against 74.7 for LoRA; 13B, 81.5 against 80.5. Merged after training, so no inference cost.
  • Treating the norm as a constant in backpropagation cut memory by 24.4% on LLaMA-7B for 0.2 points of accuracy.
Liu et al. 2024 (DoRA), arXiv:2402.09353 §3–5, Table 1, Table 7 · bibliography

  • LoRA at common ranks fell well short of full fine-tuning on code and math continued pre-training; on instruction tuning, r = 256 closed most of the gap.
  • Full fine-tuning learned weight changes of high rank: the rank needed for 90% of their variance was 10–100× typical LoRA ranks, and it grew during training.
  • LoRA regularized better than weight decay or dropout: at r = 256 it learned as much as they did and forgot less.

Training impact and afterlife

  • Fine-tuning moved to one GPU. QLoRA made 65B-class fine-tuning possible on a single 48 GB card, and 4-bit base plus 16-bit adapters became a common default for small teams.
  • Practical settings (Biderman et al.): LoRA for instruction tuning rather than continued pre-training; adapt all linear layers; r up to 256 if memory allows; α = 2r; a learning rate about 10× higher than for full fine-tuning, swept between 10−5 and 5 × 10−4.
  • Choose by what must not be lost. Teaching new knowledge (a language, a code base) needs full fine-tuning or a high rank; changing behavior or format on a model that must keep its skills suits LoRA.
  • Quantized inference formats for serving: compute deck 14.
