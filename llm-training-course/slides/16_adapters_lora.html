Adapters, prefix tuning and LoRA

Full fine-tuning updates and stores every weight, once per task. Adapters (2019) insert small trained layers, prefix tuning (2021) trains virtual tokens, and LoRA (2021) trains a low-rank update beside each frozen matrix, then folds it back in. LoRA became the default.

Problem: one full copy per task

  • Full fine-tuning of GPT-3 175B with Adam needed 1.2 TB of GPU memory, and every task produced a new 350 GB checkpoint.
  • Serving many tasks then means many full models, or swapping them in and out.
  • Most of the model should not need to change for a new task. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning trains a small set of new or chosen parameters and freezes the rest, so optimizer state and checkpoints shrink with it.
  • Three places to put the new parameters: new layers in the stack, new inputs to attention, or a correction to existing weights.

Adapters: a bottleneck inside each layer

  • Two small modules per Transformer layer, after attention and after the feed-forward block: project d down to m, apply a nonlinearity, project back to d, add a skip connection. Near-identity initialization is needed for stable training.
  • 2md + d + m parameters per adapter; with layer norms and the classifier head trained too, 0.5–8% of the model per task.
  • Evidence: BERT-Large on GLUE, mean 80.0 against 80.4 for full fine-tuning, with 3.6% extra parameters per task; all tasks together are 1.3× the model instead of 9×.
  • Cost: the extra layers run in sequence at inference and add latency, which matters most at small batch sizes.

Prefix tuning: trained virtual tokens at every layer

  • Keep the model frozen and prepend trainable key and value vectors at every layer, which all real tokens can attend to. About 0.1% of the parameters: 250K for GPT-2 Medium on E2E.
  • Training the prefix directly was unstable; an MLP reparameterizes it during training and is dropped afterwards. Initializing from real words' activations beat random initialization.
  • Evidence: matched or beat full fine-tuning on table-to-text; on XSUM summarization ROUGE-L 35.05 against 37.25. With 50 to 500 examples it beat fine-tuning by 2.9 BLEU on average, and it extrapolated better to unseen topics.
  • Cost: the prefix takes context length; performance peaked near 200 prefix tokens for summarization and 10 for table-to-text.

LoRA: a low-rank update beside the frozen weight

ΔW = BA starts at zero because B does. The paper adapts only the attention projections Wq and Wv, with α set once and not tuned. After training, W0 + (α/r)BA is a single matrix: no extra layer and no added latency. Lab L4 writes the layer and its gradients.

Why a low rank is enough, and when it is not

  • Intrinsic rank: updates learned at r = 8 and r = 64 share their top singular direction and little else; the rest looks like training noise. That is why r = 1 works on these tasks.
  • What ΔW does: it amplifies directions that W0 already has but does not emphasize, by about 20× at r = 4 (about 2× at r = 64), rather than adding W0's top directions again.
  • The caveat, in the paper: a small r may not be enough when the task is far from pre-training, for example a different language.
  • Deck 17 measures that caveat: on code and math continued pre-training, LoRA learns less than full fine-tuning.

Check yourself

  1. Why does LoRA initialize B to zero? What would a random B do to the model at step 0?
  2. A 4,096 × 4,096 matrix gets a rank-16 LoRA. How many trainable parameters is that, and what fraction of the matrix?
  3. Adapters add latency and LoRA does not. Why can a LoRA update be merged and an adapter not?
  4. Prefix tuning beat fine-tuning with 50 examples but not with the full data. Why might a frozen model with few trained parameters do better when data is scarce?
