The training loop: AdamW and learning-rate schedules

Every pre-training run fixes three things before its first step: the loss, the optimizer and how the learning rate moves. This deck reads the papers behind today's defaults: Adam, AdamW, warm-up and cosine, and warm-up-stable-decay.

Problem: one run, no second chance

  • The loss is fixed: mean next-token cross-entropy, −log p(xt | x<t), over every position of every sequence. Masked and span-corruption objectives are in architecture deck 03 and 04; extra heads that predict further ahead are in deck 27.
  • What is left to choose is how each gradient becomes a weight update: the optimizer, its weight decay, and the learning rate at every step.
  • A frontier run is too expensive to repeat, so these choices are made on small runs and must still hold at full size. And a cosine schedule needs the run's length up front: stop early or continue, and it no longer fits.

Method 1: Adam

Per element, with gradient gt and m0 = v0 = 0:

mt = β1 mt−1 + (1 − β1) gt  ·  vt = β2 vt−1 + (1 − β2) gt2
m̂t = mt / (1 − β1t)  ·  v̂t = vt / (1 − β2t)  ·  θt = θt−1 − α m̂t / (√v̂t + ε)

  • Defaults in the paper: α = 0.001, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, ε = 10−8.
  • Bias correction: both averages start at zero, so early on they underestimate the moments. Without it, early steps with β2 near 1 are much too large; the paper shows training becoming unstable.
  • Each weight gets its own step size: about α in magnitude, whatever the gradient's scale.

Method 2: AdamW decouples weight decay

  • For SGD, L2 regularization and weight decay are the same thing once the coefficient is scaled by the learning rate.
  • For Adam they are not: an L2 term enters the gradient and is divided by √v̂ with it, so weights with large past gradients are regularized less (Proposition 2).
  • AdamW applies the decay outside the adaptive step:

θt = θt−1 − ηt ( α m̂t / (√v̂t + ε) + λ θt−1 )

  • Evidence, on image classification: decoupled decay beat L2 under every schedule tried, and made the best learning rate and weight decay more independent of each other. The authors ask for checks on other tasks.
Loshchilov and Hutter 2017, arXiv:1711.05101 §2, Algorithm 2, §4.1–4.2, §5 · bibliography

Method 3: warm-up, then a schedule

Warm-up ramps the rate from near zero while Adam's second-moment estimates settle. Cosine needs the total step count in advance. WSD holds the peak and decays only at the end.

Method 4: warm-up-stable-decay (WSD)

  • MiniCPM splits the schedule into three phases: linear warm-up to the peak η, a stable phase at η, and a decay f(s − T) over the last steps, with 0 < f ≤ 1 decreasing.
  • A decay over about 10% of the total tokens was enough for their best results; 2.5% fell short. Their production run used an exponential decay.
  • During the decay the loss drops sharply, even though the weights move less than in the stable phase.
  • Any stable-phase checkpoint can be decayed to give a finished model, and the run can continue from before the decay.
Hu et al. 2024 (MiniCPM), arXiv:2404.06395 §4.2–4.4, Eq. 1, §6.2 · bibliography

Training impact and afterlife

  • Length is no longer fixed in advance. A WSD run can be extended, branched into several cool-downs, or stopped at a checkpoint and decayed.
  • Cheaper scaling laws. One long run plus cool-downs replaces one cosine run per token budget. MiniCPM used this to estimate about 192 tokens per parameter for its setup, against Chinchilla's 20 (architecture deck 06); the authors call such estimates approximate.
  • Mid-training as a stage. OLMo 2 decays the rate linearly to zero over 5–10% of training FLOPs on a higher-quality mix, then averages several such runs (deck 08).
  • AdamW remains the default; deck 04 reads Muon, the alternative used to train Moonlight and Kimi K2.

Check yourself

  1. Why does dividing an L2 term by √v̂ weaken regularization on weights with large gradients?
  2. After Adam's first step with bias correction, how far has each weight moved?
  3. You must decide a run's length after it starts. Which schedule do you pick, and what do you do at the end?
