Evaluating LLMs: benchmarks, judges, arenas and contamination

Every stage of this course ends with an evaluation that says whether it worked. This deck covers the main kinds: multiple-choice knowledge (MMLU, 2020), code checked by unit tests (HumanEval, 2021), many metrics across many scenarios (HELM, 2022), open-ended answers judged by a model or by people (MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, 2023 and 2024), and how to tell whether a model has seen the test (2023).

Problem: what does a score mean?

  • Coverage: one benchmark measures one skill; models were each reported on different sets of tasks.
  • Open-ended answers have no single right string, so exact-match scoring fails for chat.
  • Sampling: a model that solves a problem once in ten tries is neither right nor wrong; the estimator matters.
  • Contamination: benchmarks are on the web, and web text is pre-training data (deck 05). A high score may be memory.

MMLU: knowledge across 57 subjects

  • 15,908 four-way multiple-choice questions over 57 subjects, from elementary mathematics to law and medicine; random chance is 25%, and expert-level accuracy is estimated at about 89.8%.
  • GPT-3 175B, few-shot: 43.9%. UnifiedQA (11B, no further fine-tuning): 48.9%.
  • Lopsided: GPT-3 ranged from about 69% on US foreign policy to 26% on college chemistry, near chance on subjects such as morality and law.
  • Badly calibrated: its confidence differed from its accuracy by up to 24% zero-shot.
  • MMLU became the standard knowledge score; scores near the ceiling now say little, and its questions are widely copied online.
Hendrycks et al. 2020 (MMLU), arXiv:2009.03300 §3–4 · bibliography

HumanEval and pass@k

  • 164 hand-written Python problems with an average of 7.7 unit tests each; an answer counts only if it passes them.
  • pass@k: the chance that at least one of k samples passes. Sample n ≥ k per problem, count c correct, and estimate
pass@k = 1 − C(n − c, k) / C(n, k)
  • This is unbiased: the fraction of size-k subsets with a pass. The plug-in 1 − (1 − c/n)k underestimates it. Chen et al. used n = 200 for k up to 100.
  • Lab L9 checks both against brute-force enumeration.
Chen et al. 2021 (Codex, HumanEval), arXiv:2107.03374 §2, App. A · Lab L9 · bibliography

HELM: many metrics, many scenarios

  • Metrics: accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity and efficiency, measured for each core scenario where possible (87.5% of the time).
  • Coverage: before HELM, models had been evaluated on 17.9% of its core scenarios on average; HELM raised that to 96.0%.
  • Plus 7 targeted evaluations over 26 further scenarios. All prompts and outputs were released.
  • Lesson: a single leaderboard number hides trade-offs between metrics, and comparisons need the same prompts.
Liang et al. 2022 (HELM), arXiv:2211.09110 Abstract, §1 · bibliography

LLM-as-a-judge: MT-Bench

  • 80 multi-turn questions in 8 categories (writing, reasoning, math, coding and others); a strong model such as GPT-4 grades the answers or picks the better of two.
  • Agreement: GPT-4 agreed with human experts on about 85% of non-tied votes; humans agreed with each other on about 81%.
  • Biases: position (most judges favour the first answer; only GPT-4 was consistent in more than 60% of swapped pairs), verbosity (longer answers preferred), and possibly self-enhancement.
  • Fixes: judge both orders and count only consistent wins; give a reference answer for math, which cut the failure rate from 70% to 15%.
Zheng et al. 2023 (MT-Bench), arXiv:2306.05685 §3–4 · bibliography

Chatbot Arena: ratings from pairwise votes

  • Users chat with two anonymous models and vote for the better answer: about 240K votes from over 90K users, more than 50 models, over 100 languages, by January 2024.
  • Bradley-Terry, not online Elo: fit all votes at once by maximum likelihood, with P(i beats j) = σ(si − sj). The fit does not depend on vote order and gives confidence intervals.
  • Active sampling: choose the next pair to shrink the intervals most; on a replay, 4,400 votes instead of 6,800 reached the same precision.
  • Quality: crowd votes agreed with experts 72.8% to 83.1% of the time; experts agreed with each other 79.4% to 89.8%.

Contamination: a test from example order

  • Benchmark examples are exchangeable: with no contamination, a model gives the published order no more likelihood than any shuffled order.
  • A model trained on the benchmark file in its published order prefers that order. Compare the canonical log-likelihood with shuffled ones: a permutation test with a guaranteed false-positive rate.
  • Needs only log-probabilities, not the training data or the weights. A sharded version splits the data set and combines the shards with a t-test, for power at low cost.
  • Limits: only verbatim copies in the canonical order; exchangeability must hold; no correction for testing many benchmarks.
Oren et al. 2023, arXiv:2310.17623 §2–3 · Lab L9 · bibliography

Evidence: contamination

  • Planted copies: a 1.4B GPT-2-style model trained on Wikitext plus 10 benchmarks, each copied 1 to 100 times (under 0.1% of the corpus).
  • One copy was not reliably detected (p = 0.16 to 0.48); detection began around four copies; ten copies gave p = 2 × 10−11 or smaller.
  • Public models (LLaMA2-7B, Mistral-7B, Pythia-1.4B, GPT-2 XL, BioMedLM): no evidence of widespread verbatim contamination; ARC on Mistral was borderline (p = 0.001). MMLU gave weak heuristic evidence for LLaMA2 and Mistral (p ≈ 0.01).
  • Paraphrased or reordered leaks need other checks, such as n-gram overlap with the training data, which only the trainer has.

Training impact and afterlife

  • Evaluations decide stages: pre-training ends on loss and knowledge scores, SFT on instruction-following, RL on pass@k and on judged quality; deck 33 asks you to plan these gates.
  • Decontaminate before training, by n-gram filtering of test sets from the corpus, and keep held-out sets that never leave the team.
  • Judges and arenas are themselves trained against: length and style can raise a judge's score without better answers (length bias, decks 20 and 23).
  • Report the estimator: temperature and n for pass@k, both orders for judges, intervals for ratings.
