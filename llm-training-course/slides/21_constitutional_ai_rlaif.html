AI feedback: Constitutional AI and RLAIF

Human comparisons are slow and costly, and labelers asked only for harmlessness rewarded evasive answers. Constitutional AI (2022) replaced the human harmlessness labels with a model's judgements guided by a short list of written principles. RLAIF (2023) compared AI and human labels head to head and found them about equal.

Problem: harmlessness labels are costly and push toward evasion

  • Anthropic's HH models (deck 20) needed tens of thousands of red-teaming comparisons, and people had to read harmful content to make them.
  • Labels that reward the less harmful answer favour refusals; the HH policies became evasive on sensitive questions.
  • The labelers' reasons stay implicit in their choices; nobody can read or change the objective.
  • Idea: write the objective as principles in plain language, and let a model apply them to produce the training data.
Bai et al. 2022 (Constitutional AI), arXiv:2212.08073 §1 · Bai et al. 2022 (Anthropic HH), arXiv:2204.05862 §4.4 · bibliography

Stage 1, SL-CAI: the model revises its own answers

  • 16 principles for harmlessness, one sampled at random for each critique, with a few examples of critiques and revisions in the prompt.
  • The pre-trained model is fine-tuned for one epoch on the revisions plus helpfulness samples, so it does not forget how to help.
  • Harmlessness rose with each revision; critiquing before revising helped small models and made little difference for large ones.
Bai et al. 2022 (Constitutional AI), arXiv:2212.08073 §3.1–3.5, Figs. 5, 7 · bibliography

Stage 2, RL-CAI: AI labels for the preference model

  • The feedback model reads a principle and two answers and answers (A) or (B); its normalized probabilities are soft labels, ensembled over the 16 principles.
  • The preference model trained on 182,831 AI harmlessness comparisons and 135,296 human helpfulness comparisons. Only helpfulness still used human labels.
  • With chain of thought ("Let's think step by step") the labels were more accurate but nearly 0 or 1; clamping them to 40–60% worked better than 20–80% for RL.
Bai et al. 2022 (Constitutional AI), arXiv:2212.08073 §4.1–4.3 · bibliography

Evidence: Constitutional AI

  • At 52B, RL-CAI was more harmless than HH RLHF at similar helpfulness, judged by crowdworkers' Elo scores. SL-CAI alone was more harmless than a helpful-only RLHF model but less than HH RLHF.
  • RL-CAI was almost never evasive: it engaged with harmful requests and explained why it would not help.
  • The feedback model's accuracy on the HHH evaluations rose with model size, and chain of thought raised it further; the trend suggested that larger models would match preference models trained on human labels.
  • Goodhart: trained too long, RL-CAI became preachy or harsh and added boilerplate ("you are valid, valued and cared for").
Bai et al. 2022 (Constitutional AI), arXiv:2212.08073 §2, §3.3, §4.3–4.4, Figs. 2, 4, 8 · bibliography

Evidence: what makes an AI labeler good

  • Alignment with people on held-out human preferences: 78.0% for summarization with a detailed prompt and chain of thought, 69.1% for helpfulness, 72.1% for harmlessness. People agreed with each other 73–77% of the time on the summarization data.
  • Labeler size: 78.0% for PaLM 2 L, 73.8% for S and 62.7% for XS. Chain of thought generally helped; few-shot examples helped only harmlessness.
  • Self-improvement: with a labeler the same size as the policy, RLAIF still beat SFT 68% of the time.
  • Direct RLAIF skips the reward model and asks the labeler for a 1–10 score during RL: 74% against SFT on summarization.
  • Estimated labeling cost: about $0.06 per example for AI against $0.67 for people.
Lee et al. 2023 (RLAIF), arXiv:2309.00267 §2.2.2, §4.2–4.5, Tables 2–3, App. L · bibliography

Training impact and afterlife

  • AI feedback became routine: LLM judges label preferences for DPO data (Tülu 3, deck 15) and grade answers in evaluations (MT-Bench, deck 32).
  • Written principles make the objective inspectable and editable; Anthropic's constitution and model specifications from other labs follow this idea, and deliberative alignment trains models to reason over a written spec (deck 31).
  • The judge's biases transfer: position bias, a preference for longer answers and for its own style all enter the reward; RLAIF's order swapping is a minimum.
  • Human labels did not disappear: they calibrate judges and cover what a judge cannot yet do.
Bai et al. 2022 (Constitutional AI), arXiv:2212.08073 §6 · Lee et al. 2023 (RLAIF), arXiv:2309.00267 §5–7 · bibliography
