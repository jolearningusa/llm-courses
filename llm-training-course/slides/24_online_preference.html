Online and iterative preference learning; DPO against PPO

DPO trains on fixed pairs, usually written by other models. Self-Rewarding Language Models (2024) let the policy write its own answers and judge them, then ran DPO again, three times. Two 2024 studies then compared DPO with well-tuned PPO and asked what actually matters: the data, the algorithm, the reward model or the prompts.

Problem: offline pairs come from someone else

  • DPO's pairs are fixed before training. As the policy moves, it writes answers unlike any in the data, and nothing tells it whether they are good.
  • PPO samples from the current policy and scores each sample with a reward model: on-policy by construction, but heavy (deck 19).
  • Middle ground: iterate. Sample from the current model, label the new answers (people, a reward model, or the model itself), run DPO, repeat. Anthropic HH and Llama 2 already collected comparisons this way for RLHF (deck 20).

Self-Rewarding Language Models

  • Llama 2 70B fine-tuned on 3,200 Open Assistant instruction examples and 1,630 examples of judging answers: model M1.
  • For new prompts, Mt samples 4 answers and scores each with an LLM-as-a-judge prompt that adds up to 5 points; the best and worst become a pair, ties dropped. DPO on 3,964 pairs gave M2, on 6,942 pairs M3.
  • The judge improves along with the policy, because both are the same model.

Is DPO superior to PPO? The theory

  • Theorem: every policy PPO can reach is a DPO solution, but not the reverse. DPO's loss only sees answers in the data, so it can put probability on answers the reference never produces.
  • A three-answer example: a DPO solution gives 0.9 to an answer with zero reference probability; PPO's KL penalty forbids it.
  • Distribution shift on SafeRLHF (Llama 2 7B): DPO from an Alpaca SFT model reached a 55.4% safety rate; first fine-tuning on the preference data's own answers raised it to 71.8%; iterative DPO reached 99.9%, still with negative helpfulness; PPO 99.5% with positive helpfulness.
  • On APPS, swapping DPO's reference for the pre-trained CodeLlama dropped it from 12.8% to 0.24%; PPO moved from 15.1% to 13.8%.
Xu et al. 2024, arXiv:2404.10719 §4, Thm. 4.1, Tables 1–2, 9 · bibliography

Evidence: PPO, tuned, wins on hard tasks

  • What PPO needed: advantage normalization, large batches and an exponential moving average of the reference model. Together they raised CodeContests pass@1k from 7.7% to 21.4%.
  • CodeContests (CodeLlama 34B): PPO 22.4% on the 10@1k metric, above AlphaCode-41B's 16.4%. DPO 0.0%, iterative DPO 3.2%.
  • Dialogue: on HH-RLHF, OpenAssistant reward 0.718 for PPO against 0.611 for DPO; GPT-4 preferred PPO 42 times, tied 28 and preferred DPO 30.

Training impact and afterlife

  • On-policy data matters more than the loss. Recipes moved to DPO on the model's own samples, labeled by a judge or reward model, in several rounds; Tülu 3 built its DPO pairs from on-policy and off-policy answers (deck 15).
  • DPO for cost, PPO for the last points: DPO is the cheap default; PPO-style RL with a reward model or a verifier is used where the gains matter most, such as math and code.
  • That second road leads to Part IX: RL with verifiable rewards, where GRPO drops PPO's value model (deck 26) and a program, not a learned model, gives the reward (deck 27).
  • Judges that grade their own answers inherit their own biases, length first (deck 32).
