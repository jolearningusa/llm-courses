Verifiers and process rewards

Math has answers that can be checked. Cobbe et al. (2021) trained a verifier to pick the best of 100 sampled solutions; STaR (2022) fine-tuned a model on its own solutions that reached the right answer; Lightman et al. (2023) showed that rewarding each step beats rewarding the final answer; Math-Shepherd (2023) labeled steps automatically. These are the pieces RL for reasoning is built from.

Problem: one wrong step spoils a solution

  • Multi-step math fails in a way next-token training does not see: a single wrong step leads to a wrong answer, and the model cannot tell.
  • Scaling fine-tuning alone was slow. On GSM8K, fine-tuned GPT-3 needed far more parameters for each gain.
  • But the final answer can be checked. That gives three tools: rank sampled solutions by a learned verifier, train on solutions that check out, and reward the steps that lead there.
Cobbe et al. 2021 (GSM8K verifiers), arXiv:2110.14168 §1, §2 · bibliography

GSM8K and trained verifiers

  • GSM8K: 8.5K grade-school word problems (7.5K train, 1K test), 2 to 8 steps each.
  • Verifier: sample 100 solutions per problem, score each with a model trained to predict whether a solution reaches the right final answer, return the top one. Labels come only from the final answer, so lucky wrong reasoning counts as correct.
  • A verifier that predicts after every token beat one that predicts only at the end, which overfit.
  • Evidence: a 6B model with verification slightly beat a fine-tuned 175B model, roughly the gain of a 30× larger model; verification helped little with small data but scaled better with more.
  • More samples helped up to about 400, then hurt: the verifier was fooled by solutions that looked right.
Cobbe et al. 2021 (GSM8K verifiers), arXiv:2110.14168 §2, §4–6, Figs. 5–7 · bibliography

STaR: train on your own correct reasoning

  • Keep only rationales whose answer is correct; for problems the model missed, give it the answer as a hint, generate a rationale, and train on it without the hint (rationalization).
  • Evidence: GPT-J 6B on CommonsenseQA: 72.5% against 60.0% for direct fine-tuning and 73.0% for fine-tuned GPT-3, a model 30× larger. GSM8K: 10.7% against 5.8% for direct fine-tuning.
  • This is rejection sampling with a correctness check, the same filter Llama 2 used with a reward model (deck 20) and R1 used to build its SFT data (deck 27).
Zelikman et al. 2022 (STaR), arXiv:2203.14465 §3, Tables 1–2 · bibliography

Outcome against process supervision

  • Outcome reward model (ORM): trained on whether the final answer is right. Process reward model (PRM): trained on whether each step is right.
  • PRM800K: about 800K human labels (positive, negative or neutral) on the steps of 75K solutions to 12K MATH problems, from models fine-tuned from GPT-4.
  • Labelers saw mostly convincing wrong-answer solutions, chosen by the current PRM: active learning, about 2.6× more data-efficient than uniform choice.
  • A solution's PRM score is the product of the probabilities that each step is correct.
Lightman et al. 2023 (Let's Verify Step by Step), arXiv:2305.20050 §2, §4.2 · bibliography

  • The gap widened as more samples were ranked: a step-level verifier is harder to fool with a right answer reached the wrong way.

Math-Shepherd: step labels without people

  • For each step, a "completer" model finishes the solution N times from there. Hard estimate: the step is good if any completion reaches the right answer. Soft estimate: the fraction that do. N = 8 with LLemma-7B.
  • As a verifier reranking 256 candidates: DeepSeek-67B reached 93.3% on GSM8K and 48.1% on MATH500 (with self-consistency).
  • As a reward for PPO, step by step: Mistral-7B from 77.9% to 84.1% on GSM8K and from 28.6% to 33.0% on MATH; 89.1% and 43.5% with verification on top.
  • On MATH its automatic labels trained a better PRM than PRM800K, partly because PRM800K's solutions come from a different model.
Wang et al. 2023 (Math-Shepherd), arXiv:2312.08935 §3.3–5, Tables 1–3 · bibliography

Training impact and afterlife

  • Checked answers became the reward. GRPO (deck 26) and RLVR (deck 27) use the final-answer check directly as the reward, with no learned verifier to fool.
  • Process rewards split the field. DeepSeekMath tried PRM-based GRPO; DeepSeek-R1 used only rule-based rewards for reasoning, and Kimi k1.5 states that it needed no PRM (deck 27).
  • Verifiers moved to test time: best-of-N and search against a PRM are ways to spend inference compute (deck 28).
  • Self-training loops like STaR became rejection-sampling fine-tuning, a standard stage of reasoning pipelines.
