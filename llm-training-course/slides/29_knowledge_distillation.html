Knowledge distillation: Hinton et al., sequence-level KD, DistilBERT

A large model or an ensemble is accurate but expensive to serve. Distillation trains a smaller student on the teacher's output distribution instead of on one-hot labels. Hinton et al. (2015) gave the temperature recipe, Kim and Rush (2016) extended it to whole sequences, and DistilBERT (2019) applied it to pre-training a smaller BERT.

Problem: one-hot labels throw information away

  • Ensembles and very large models win benchmarks, but are too slow or too costly for many deployments.
  • A trained model's output says more than its top answer: the relative probabilities of the wrong classes show which classes it finds similar, for example that a 2 can look like a 3 or a 7.
  • A one-hot label carries none of that, and at temperature 1 the teacher's wrong-class probabilities are often so small that they barely move a cross-entropy loss.
  • Goal: transfer the teacher's function, including how it generalizes, not just its labels on the training set.

Soft targets at a temperature

pi = exp(vi/T) / Σj exp(vj/T)
L = α T² CE(p, q) + (1 − α) CE(y, softmax(z))
  • Both models use the same high T in the soft term; the student uses T = 1 at test time.
  • The soft gradient is (qi − pi)/T and shrinks like 1/T², so the soft term is multiplied by T² to keep its weight when T changes.

Matching logits is the high-temperature limit

∂CE/∂zi ≈ (zi − vi) / (N T²)
  • At high T, with zero-mean logits over N classes, distillation becomes minimizing the squared distance between logits, the method of Caruana and colleagues.
  • At lower T the student pays little attention to logits far below the mean. Those are almost unconstrained by the teacher's training, so they can be noisy.
  • When the student is much too small to capture all the teacher's knowledge, intermediate temperatures worked best, which suggests that ignoring the very negative logits helps.
Hinton et al. 2015, arXiv:1503.02531 §2.1 · Lab L8 (gradient table in kd.py) · bibliography

Sequence-level KD

  • Word-level KD: match the teacher's next-token distribution at each position of the gold translation (teacher forcing).
  • Sequence-level KD: approximate the teacher's distribution over whole sequences by its mode: run beam search with the teacher and train the student on that output as if it were the label.
  • Sequence-level interpolation: pick, among the teacher's beam, the candidate closest to the gold sequence, and train on it.
  • The student is trained on what the teacher would say, not on what a human wrote: the same move as SFT on a strong model's outputs (deck 14) and R1's distilled models (deck 30).
Kim and Rush 2016, arXiv:1606.07947 §3 · bibliography

DistilBERT: distillation during pre-training

  • Student: BERT with 6 layers instead of 12; token-type embeddings and the pooler removed; initialized from every other layer of the teacher.
  • Loss: Lce on the teacher's soft masked-token distribution, plus the usual masked-LM loss Lmlm, plus Lcos aligning the student's and teacher's hidden states.
  • Result: 66M parameters against 110M, 60% faster at inference (410 against 668 s), and 97% of BERT-base's GLUE score (77.0 against 79.5). Training took 90 hours on 8 V100 GPUs.

Training impact and afterlife

  • Soft targets are data: a teacher gives a full distribution per token, far more signal per example than a label, so a student can learn from fewer examples.
  • Two families survive: logit distillation, which needs the teacher's probabilities and a shared vocabulary (DistilBERT, Gemma 2 in deck 30), and sequence-level distillation, which needs only the teacher's text (instruction data in deck 14, R1's distilled models).
  • Initialization from the teacher matters as much as the loss for a pre-trained student.
  • Open problem: both train on fixed sequences, but the student then generates from its own prefixes. Deck 30 trains on the student's own samples instead.
