GPT-3 and the scaling laws

Kaplan et al. (January 2020) measured how loss falls with model size, data and compute. Brown et al. (May 2020) bet on that curve with a 175B-parameter decoder and found in-context learning.

Problem: how big, and on how much data?

  • GPT-2 showed that zero-shot ability rose with size, and that 1.5B parameters still underfit WebText.
  • Training runs were expensive. Without a predictive rule, choosing the next model size, data size and number of steps was guesswork.
  • Kaplan et al. trained hundreds of decoder-only models, from about 768 to 1.5B non-embedding parameters, to fit that rule.

Kaplan: three power laws

Limited byLawExponent
Parameters N (non-embedding)L(N) = (Nc / N)αNαN ≈ 0.076
Data D (tokens)L(D) = (Dc / D)αDαD ≈ 0.095
Compute C (optimally allocated)L(Cmin) = (Cc / Cmin)αCαC ≈ 0.050
  • Each law is a straight line on log-log axes over many orders of magnitude.
  • Training compute is about C ≈ 6N per token: 2N for the forward pass and 4N for the backward pass.

Kaplan: shape barely matters, size does

  • "Performance depends strongly on scale, weakly on model shape": depth versus width, heads and FFN ratio change the loss little over a wide range.
  • Larger models are more sample-efficient: they reach a given loss with fewer tokens.
  • For a fixed compute budget, the fit says to grow parameters fastest: Nopt ∝ C0.73, data only D ∝ C0.27, and stop well before convergence.
  • That allocation is the one Chinchilla (deck 06) overturned.

Change: learning from the context, not the gradient

  • K is typically 10 to 100: as many examples as fit in 2,048 tokens.
  • The task is defined entirely by how the context is built. This is why later parts of the course care so much about the context window and its construction.
Brown et al. 2020, arXiv:2005.14165 §2, Figure 2.1 · bibliography
6 / 10

Evidence

  • Few-shot TriviaQA (closed book): 71.2%, above the fine-tuned closed-book state of the art of the time.
  • Few-shot LAMBADA: 86.4%, more than 18 points above the previous best.
  • The gap between zero-shot and few-shot widens with size: larger models make better use of examples in the context.
  • Weak spots the authors report: some reading comprehension and natural language inference tasks, and benchmark contamination, which they measured and discussed.
Brown et al. 2020, arXiv:2005.14165 §3, §4, Figure 1.2 · bibliography
7 / 10

Modeling impact

  • Scale as the main lever. The architecture is GPT-2's. The change is size, data and compute, guided by a fitted curve.
  • In-context learning. One model, many tasks, no fine-tuning. Prompts became the interface; prompt length became a cost.
  • The parameter count C ≈ 6ND became the standard back-of-envelope for training cost, and 2N FLOPs per token for inference.
  • Inference memory. At 96 layers and d = 12,288, each token's K and V take 2 × 96 × 12,288 ≈ 2.4M values, about 4.7 MB in 16-bit. That is the pressure MQA and GQA relieve (decks 12–13).

Afterlife

  • The sparse-attention layers were not adopted by most successors; dense attention with FlashAttention (deck 14) and later learned sparsity (deck 26) took their place.
  • Kaplan's allocation was replaced two years later by Chinchilla's roughly equal scaling of parameters and tokens (deck 06).
  • In-context learning, few-shot prompting and the 2,048-token window set the expectations that instruction tuning and chat templates (deck 11) built on.
  • The power-law method itself endured: later reports still fit scaling curves to choose model and data size.
