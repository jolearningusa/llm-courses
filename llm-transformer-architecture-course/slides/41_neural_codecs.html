SoundStream and EnCodec

Zeghidour et al. (Google, 2021) and Défossez et al. (Meta, 2022). A convolutional encoder, a residual vector quantizer and a decoder, trained end to end with adversarial losses, compress audio to a few kbps. The quantizer's codes also became the "tokens" that audio language models generate.

Problem: low bitrates, and a vocabulary for audio

  • Classic codecs (Opus, EVS) rely on hand-designed signal processing; at low bitrates they lose quality quickly.
  • Earlier neural vocoders could decode compact features, but were not trained end to end as codecs and did not scale bitrate in one model.
  • For language modeling, raw audio is a poor vocabulary: 24,000 samples per second of continuous values. A good codec turns it into tens of discrete tokens per frame at 50–75 frames per second.

Change: encoder, RVQ, decoder

  • 24,000 / 320 = 75 frames per second in both SoundStream and EnCodec. SoundStream's convolutions are causal, so it streams.
  • Training losses: reconstruction (multi-scale spectral), adversarial (waveform and STFT discriminators) and discriminator feature matching.

Change: residual vector quantization

One codebook would need 2⁸⁰ entries for 80 bits per frame; 8 residual codebooks of 1,024 do it with 8,192 vectors. Quantizer dropout: SoundStream samples how many codebooks to use at each training step, so one model serves 3 to 18 kbps by keeping the first n codes.

SoundStream: evidence

  • At 3 kbps SoundStream beats Opus at 6 kbps and EVS at 5.9 kbps in listening tests; the classic codecs need 12 kbps (Opus) and 9.6 kbps (EVS) to match it.
  • The bitrate-scalable model matches models trained for one bitrate at 6 and 12 kbps, and is only slightly worse at 3 kbps.
  • It runs in real time on one CPU thread of a Pixel 4 phone (more than 2.3× real time).
  • A FiLM conditioning signal switches denoising on or off, so one model compresses and enhances speech at once.
Zeghidour et al. 2021, arXiv:2107.03312 §V-A to §V-E · bibliography

EnCodec: changes and evidence

  • Adds a two-layer LSTM after the convolutions; 24 kHz mono and 48 kHz stereo models; up to 32 codebooks of 1,024; 1.5, 3, 6, 12 and 24 kbps at 24 kHz.
  • One multi-scale STFT discriminator (five window sizes), and a loss balancer that sets each loss's weight as the fraction of the total gradient it should contribute.
  • A small Transformer LM over the codes plus arithmetic coding cuts the bitrate by another 25–40%.

Modeling impact

  • Audio gets a tokenizer. RVQ codes at 75 Hz are what AudioLM, VALL-E and later speech LMs predict (deck 42), as BPE tokens are for text (deck 07).
  • Codes come in layers. The first codebook carries most of the signal and later ones add detail; generators exploit this by predicting coarse codes first (deck 42).
  • The codec sets the ceiling. A speech LM cannot sound better than its decoder, as Chameleon's images cannot beat its VQ tokenizer (deck 37).
  • Token rate is the cost. 75 frames × 8 codebooks = 600 tokens per second of audio, far more than text; this drives the hierarchical designs in deck 42.
