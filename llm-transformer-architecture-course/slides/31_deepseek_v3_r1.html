DeepSeek-V3 and DeepSeek-R1

DeepSeek-AI, December 2024 and January 2025. V3 puts together MLA, fine-grained MoE with loss-free balancing, multi-token prediction and FP8 training into a 671B model trained for 2.8M GPU hours. R1 shows that reinforcement learning with rule-based rewards alone can teach that model to reason.

Problem: frontier quality on a fixed GPU budget

  • DeepSeek trained on 2,048 H800 GPUs. Inside a node NVLink gives 160 GB/s; between nodes InfiniBand gives 50 GB/s. For a large MoE, whose tokens travel to experts on other nodes, communication rather than arithmetic is the limit.
  • Goal: a model as strong as the best closed models, with low cost for both training and serving.
  • Every piece of V3 is aimed at one of those costs: KV-cache memory (MLA), active compute (MoE), training signal per token (MTP), and arithmetic and communication (FP8, DualPipe).

Change: balance without an auxiliary loss, and MTP

  • Loss-free balancing (deck 22): each expert has a bias that is added to its score only for top-K selection. After each step it moves by γ = 0.001 against the expert's load.
  • A small sequence-wise balance loss (α = 0.0001) only guards against extreme imbalance inside one sequence.
  • No tokens are dropped during training or inference.
  • Multi-token prediction (deck 27): one extra sequential module predicts the token after next. It densifies the training signal; at inference it can be dropped or used for speculative decoding.

Change: FP8 and DualPipe

  • V3 is one of the first very large models trained with FP8 matrix multiplies; against a BF16 baseline the relative loss error stays below 0.25%.
  • DualPipe schedules forward and backward chunks so that MoE all-to-all communication hides behind computation.
  • Pre-training ran on 14.8T tokens with no irrecoverable loss spikes and no rollbacks.

R1-Zero: reasoning from RL alone

  • Start from the V3 base model with no SFT. Train with GRPO; the reward is rule-based: is the final answer correct, and is the reasoning inside the required tags.
  • AIME 2024 pass@1 rises from 15.6% to 77.9% during training (86.7% with majority voting).
  • Responses grow longer on their own. The model starts to re-check its work: an "aha moment" where it writes "wait" and revisits a step.
  • Problems: poor readability and mixing of languages in one answer.

R1: the full pipeline

R1: AIME 2024 79.8%, MATH-500 97.3%, Codeforces rating 2029, GPQA-Diamond 71.5%. The language-consistency reward is the share of chain-of-thought words in the target language. R1's outputs were distilled into Qwen2.5 and Llama 3 models of 1.5B to 70B parameters.

Modeling impact

  • Architecture as cost engineering. Every V3 choice traces back to a hardware bottleneck: memory, bandwidth or arithmetic.
  • The cost of a frontier run fell. 2.8M GPU hours made other labs revisit their budgets, and MLA, loss-free balancing and FP8 spread quickly (Kimi K2 copies the V3 layout, deck 33).
  • Reasoning is trained, not prompted. R1 made RL with verifiable rewards the standard way to build reasoning models; Qwen3 and others followed (deck 30).
  • Long outputs move cost to inference. A model that thinks for thousands of tokens shifts spending from training to serving, which raises the value of MLA and MTP.

Check yourself

  1. Why does limiting each token to 4 nodes matter more on H800 than it would on hardware with higher interconnect bandwidth?
  2. The balancing bias changes which experts are chosen but not the gating weights. Why does that matter for the model's output?
  3. R1-Zero uses only correctness and format rewards. Why is that harder to "reward hack" than a learned reward model?
  4. V3's MLA caches 512 latent values plus a 64-dimensional RoPE key per layer (deck 16). Over 61 layers in BF16, compare that with lab L5's kv_bytes_per_token for GQA with 8 KV heads of 128.
