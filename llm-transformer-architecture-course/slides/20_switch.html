Problem: MoE was complex, costly to communicate and unstable

  • Earlier MoE work routed each token to k ≥ 2 experts, on the intuition that a router needs to compare at least two experts to learn.
  • MoE models were hard to train in low precision, communicated heavily between devices, and had not displaced dense models such as T5.
  • Question: what is the simplest sparse layer that keeps the gains?

Change: top-1 routing

  • One expert per token: less router computation, half the minimum expert capacity of top-2, and simpler communication.
  • The authors found that k = 1 kept model quality.

Expert capacity and dropped tokens

capacity = (tokens per batch ÷ experts) × capacity factor. Tokens beyond an expert's capacity skip the layer through the residual connection. The paper compares factors of 1.0, 1.25 and 2.0; with the balance loss, fewer than 1% of tokens are typically dropped.

Change: a simpler balance loss

loss = α · N · Σi fi · Pi , α = 10−2

  • fi: the fraction of tokens dispatched to expert i (not differentiable). Pi: the mean router probability for expert i (differentiable).
  • Under uniform routing fi = Pi = 1/N and the sum times N equals 1, its minimum. Concentrating tokens on few experts raises it.
  • The authors swept α from 10−1 to 10−5; 10−2 balanced load without hurting the main loss.

5 / 10

Change: making it train

  • Selective precision. Run the model in bfloat16 but compute the router in float32 locally: near-bfloat16 speed, stable training.
  • Smaller initialization. Scale the default weight initialization by 0.1; quality improves and variance across runs falls.
  • Expert dropout. When fine-tuning, use dropout 0.4 inside experts and 0.1 elsewhere; experts have many parameters and few examples each.

Modeling impact

  • Sparsity as a scaling axis. At fixed compute per token, adding experts kept lowering loss; parameter count and FLOPs became separate dials.
  • Stability is part of the architecture. Router precision and initialization scale are listed with the layer, not as training trivia.
  • Capacity is a trade-off. A higher capacity factor drops fewer tokens but wastes compute on padding; inference wants no drops at all.
  • Encoder-decoder era. Switch was built on T5; decoder-only MoE LLMs come with Mixtral and DeepSeek (decks 21–22).

Afterlife

  • The fi · Pi balance loss became the standard form of auxiliary loss; DeepSeekMoE's expert-level balance loss has the same fi · Pi shape (deck 22).
  • Top-1 routing itself did not become the default for LLMs: Mixtral uses top-2, DeepSeek and Qwen route to 8 of many small experts, gpt-oss to 4 of 32 or 128.
  • Float32 routers and capacity factors remain standard training practice.
